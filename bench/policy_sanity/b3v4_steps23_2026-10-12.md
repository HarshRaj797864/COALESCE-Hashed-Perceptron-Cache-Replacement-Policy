## What it establishes

- **Step 2 is safe and small.** It changes nothing that agreed and moves
  barnes 50×, still far short.
- **Step 3 is the right kind of mechanism.**
  - ocean's R_b falls from 1.26 / 1.34 to 0.99 / 1.13. An upgrade leaves the
    upgrader the sole listed owner, which removes sharers listed only by an
    old read. That is the direction the design predicted.
  - canneal, which has almost no upgrades, is unchanged, and its upgrade
    count matches gem5's without being asked to.
  - barnes moves from three orders of magnitude low to the right order of
    magnitude.
- **Step 3 over-produces ownership hand-overs on barnes.** Store misses that
  find another core, plus upgrades:
  - gem5: 44,867 + 61,777 = 106,644;
  - ChampSim: 189,125 + 209,018 = 398,143, which is 3.7×.
  - Its ownership-request stream at the shared cache is 2.71× gem5's, and
    its shared-cache traffic 1.84×.
- **Step 3 under-counts upgrades on ocean**, and more at 8 MB. gem5's
  upgrade count rises 1.6× from 2 MB to 8 MB; ChampSim's falls. The cause is
  not established here.

**What the barnes rows say about the cause.** The design named two
candidates for an overshoot.
- *A sole clean copy leaving silently, so its core stays listed.* This can
  explain at most 13% of barnes's upgrades: 181,393 of the 209,018 found a
  live copy in another core's private caches, not just a stale listing.
- *A read-modify-write that is not atomic.* barnes drops 59,753 fills as
  stale, against 3 to 30 on the other four. A dropped fill means a line was
  taken between a core's request and its arrival. The read-modify-write's
  store then misses and asks again. gem5 holds the line for both halves.

**This points at the second candidate; it does not prove it.** The rows hold
no count of stores that missed after their own load had taken the line.
That counter is the next measurement.
